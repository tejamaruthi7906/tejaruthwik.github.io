Topology-Optimised Robotic Gripper

Team case study · 5 students

Design, structural validation and additive manufacturing of a lightweight, servo-actuated 4-finger gripper for 5 kg cubic payloads.

Problem / Goal
Industries handling small, dense, cube-shaped parts need a lightweight, low-tooling-cost gripper that can be adapted to different cube sizes. The brief was to design, optimise and additively manufacture a claw-type gripper that safely and repeatably handles cubic objects up to 5 kg.
My role
Design Engineer on a 5-person team: created the 3D CAD design of the gripper and ran the topology optimisation on the claw, center screw and claw-holder; updated design drawings based on functional-test feedback.
Tools & methods
SolidWorks CAD SolidWorks Simulation (static FEA) SIMP topology optimisation FDM (TPU-95A / PLA) Bambu Lab H2S Cost / break-even modelling
Approach
Designed a servo/lead-screw-driven 4-finger claw mechanism, where axial motion of a central claw-holder compresses a spring body that closes the flexible claws around the object. Started from a solid, conservatively-sized baseline claw, applied the two governing load cases (normal gripping force and vertical shear from the payload weight), then ran SIMP-based topology optimisation on the claw, center screw and claw-holder to remove low-stress material while keeping the functional interfaces untouched. The optimised geometry was re-checked with static FEA, cleaned up for FDM manufacturability, and printed in TPU-95A (flexible claws/spring) and PLA (rigid housing) — TPU-95A was chosen over softer TPU variants for its higher stiffness, easier printability, and roughly 67% lower material cost than TPU-70A.
Key results
  • 33% mass reduction versus the solid baseline design, with stresses kept within safe limits
  • Handles cubic objects up to 5 kg with stable, slip-free gripping and elastic recovery after release, confirmed in physical testing of the printed gripper
  • Estimated manufacturing cost of ≈€180 per unit on a 4-printer FDM cell, with a break-even point of ≈160 units

What I learned — translating a topology-optimisation output into a part that is actually printable and assemblable meant re-adding manufacturing constraints (minimum wall thickness, support access, functional interfaces) by hand rather than trusting the raw optimiser geometry. Running the CAD and optimisation work inside a 5-person team also meant keeping drawings traceable as the design changed in response to test feedback.

Vision-Guided AGV — Chassis Design & Simulation

Team case study · 4 students · Grade 1.0

Mechanical platform and URDF model for a differential-drive AGV, validated in a closed-loop ROS/Gazebo simulation with LQR control and OpenCV-based lane detection.

Problem / Goal
Design, model and simulate a control and sensing system for an Automated Guided Vehicle (AGV) that follows a marked path autonomously — covering the mechanical platform, the vehicle dynamics and controller, and camera-based lane detection, integrated in one Gazebo simulation.
My role
Mechanical CAD design and URDF conversion for the AGV chassis; authored the report's abstract, conclusion and results sections. Teammates derived the state-space dynamics, designed and compared the controllers, and built the OpenCV/ROS lane-detection pipeline.
Tools & methods
Fusion 360 (CAD) SolidWorks → Simscape Multibody URDF ROS Noetic Gazebo 11 MATLAB / Simulink OpenCV
Approach
Designed a differential-drive chassis — two powered front wheels, a pair of free-turning rear castors, and an aluminium cargo tray on top — modelled with defined joints and linkages between wheels and base link. Converted the CAD assembly into a URDF so the same geometry could drive both a Simscape Multibody dynamic validation and the full Gazebo simulation. That shared chassis model is what let the team's separately-developed LQR controller and OpenCV lane-detection pipeline be tested together as one closed-loop system.
Key results
  • Case study graded 1.0 (top grade) at THD
  • Open-loop system confirmed unstable (eigenvalues [0, 0.8, 0]) and cross-coupled (RGA = [[0.5, 0.5], [0.5, 0.5]]), establishing the need for a MIMO controller
  • Of four controllers compared (Pole Placement, LQR, Mixsyn, Loopsyn), LQR gave the lowest overshoot — 0.89 rad on heading — and the closest setpoint tracking
  • Simscape validation: at a commanded 0.1 m/s, the AGV reached a 90° heading change in ≈16.6 s
  • Full system in Gazebo: HSV/OpenCV lane detection plus the LQR controller aligned the robot to the path centre in ≈15 s

What I learned — a URDF is only as useful as the CAD decisions behind it — joint placement and link naming in the original assembly directly determined how clean the Simscape and Gazebo integration turned out to be. Working across mechanical, controls and computer-vision teammates also meant agreeing on a shared coordinate convention early, instead of reconciling it after each piece was already built.
